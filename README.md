# Overview
Penney’s game is a probability-based coin-flipping game in which two players take turns choosing a short sequence of coin toss outcomes—like “HHT” or “TTH.” In the Humble-Nishiyama game, instead of flipping coins as in Penney’s game, a well-shuffled deck of playing cards is used, with red cards standing in for heads and black cards for tails. Player A picks a sequence first, and Player B then selects a different sequence of the same length. Cards are revealed one by one, and the winner is the first player whose chosen color sequence appears consecutively in the stream of revealed cards. Although each possible sequence of a given length has the same basic probability of appearing overall, the order in which the sequences occur changes the odds of winning. As in Penney’s game, no matter what sequence Player A chooses, Player B can always select another sequence that gives a higher chance of appearing first. This advantage arises from overlapping patterns between sequences—Player B can pick a pattern that is more likely to occur shortly after Player A’s begins, allowing B to win more often over many rounds.
In the card-based Humble–Nishiyama version of the game, results can be measured in two different ways: by tricks or by cards. When counted by tricks, the game proceeds as follows—cards are drawn one by one until one of the players’ chosen three-color sequences appears. That player wins the “trick” and collects the cards revealed so far. The game then continues with the remaining cards in the deck, and play repeats until the deck is exhausted. The final winner is the player who has collected the most tricks overall.
This method differs from counting by cards or coin sides, as in the original Penney’s coin version, where the goal is simply to see whose sequence appears first in an ongoing sequence of independent flips. Counting by tricks means that the Humble–Nishiyama game includes multiple rounds of sequence detection rather than just one. Because each round resets and the finite deck constrains the randomness, the second player’s advantage tends to be amplified over the course of the game. In other words, when results are counted by tricks instead of individual cards or single first appearances, Player B often wins an even higher proportion of the time, especially across many tricks.
In a third variant of the Humble–Nishiyama game, scoring is based not on the number of tricks won but on the total number of cards collected. This subtle change alters the strategy for Player B. Instead of merely trying to make their sequence appear first in each round, Player B must now consider both how often they win and how long each winning round lasts. A sequence that wins slightly less often but tends to collect longer runs of cards can outperform one that wins quickly but yields fewer cards overall. As a result, simulations suggest that the optimal sequence for Player B in this version may differ from the one used in either Penney’s original coin game or the trick-based Humble–Nishiyama version.
This repository contains the code to simulate the The Humble-Nishiyama Randomness Game. It simulates every possible combination of choices, simulates games, and finds the experimental probability that a player wins with the given choices.

## Quick-Start Guide

This project utilizes UV for version control. If you are unfamiliar with UV, please see [their doccumentation](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/guides/install-python/) for more information on its implementation. Once you have downloaded the repository, simply run `uv sync` to set up all necessary packages.

Over 5 million decks have already been scored. In order to create and score more decks, run `main.py` and it will prompt you through this process. 

If you wish to completely recreate the results, delete all decks in `Decks/Scored` as well as `src/scoring/player_wins.csv` and recreate all decks.
